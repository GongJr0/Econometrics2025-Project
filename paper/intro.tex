\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
\subsection{Overview}\label{subsec:overview}
Real GDP is a prominent measure of output due to its simplicity, interpretability, and effectiveness in communicating the overall activity of an economy. 
Alongside academic settings, Real GDP has retained its relevance in politics as one of the go-to measures for public communications regarding economic growth and prosperity. 
As per its wide-spread adoption, the measure has accumulated a plethora of literature referencing it from a multitude of perspectives.

\vspace{\baselineskip}
This paper explores the link between GDP growth and theoretically grounded interactions of GDP deficit, unemployment gap, and output gap, alongside other exogenous regressors.
Ultimately, the paper aims to show that empirical observations of Real GDP growth in the U.S. economy can be modelled optimally with a \textbf{Gauss\rule[0.5ex]{0.5em}{0.7pt}Markov} compliant \textbf{Distributed Lag Model} (DLM) specification where the exogenous contributors are theoretical descriptors of output.

\subsection{Hypothesis}\label{subsec:hypothesis}
In the history of economic equilibria, multiple models have proposed mathematically defined relationships between Real GDP and many indicators.
Moreover, some interactions between said indicators have been formalized into economic models.
This paper focuses on testing the predictive and explanatory power of two such indicators involving unemployment gap, output gap, and GDP deflator ($P_t^{GDP}$):

\begin{itemize}
    \item Unemployment Gap and Output Gap (\textbf{Okun}-like): $O_t = (u_t - u^*_t) \times (Y_t - Y^*_t)$
    \item Unemployment Gap and GDP Deflator (\textbf{Phillips}-like): $\Pi_t = (u_t - u^*_t) \times \Delta\log(P_t^{GDP})$
\end{itemize}

\noindent Alongside other exogenous regressors, this paper hypothesizes that a model of the form:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:model_eq}
   \Delta \log(Y_t) = C + \sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i X_{[i, \, t-1]} + \varepsilon_t
\end{equation}

\noindent where $X_{[i, \, t-1]}$ includes the above defined interactions has an OLS estimator with no strong evidence against \textbf{BLUE}.

\vspace{\baselineskip}
By Okun's Law, we expect the output gap to be modelled by the unemployment gap and a negative coefficient.
Therefore, the interaction $O_t$ should be a redundant variable in the presence of any main-effects.
On the other hand, the $\Pi_t$ interaction is the only variable that brings an inflationary aspect into the model equation.
Given the added information, we expect $\Pi_t$ to add explanatory power though inflation while being supported by the in-set main effect of the unemployment gap.
With these theorized expectations, build upon the design hypothesis and further hypothesize that $O_t$ should have an insignificant, negative (if non-zero) coefficient while $\Pi_t$ should have a significant and negative coefficient.


\subsection{Theoretical Background}\label{subsec:theory}
We consider two equations to base our selection of interactions.
First, the \textbf{Phillips Curve} describes the relationship between inflation and unemployment.
It can be expressed in terms of expected inflation and the unemployment gap as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \pi_t = \mathbb{E}_t[\pi_{t+1}] - \alpha (u_t - u_t^*) + \varepsilon_t
    \label{eq:philips_gap}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_t$ is the inflation rate at time $t$, $\mathbb{E}_t[\pi_{t+1}]$ is the expected inflation rate at time $t+1$, $\bar{u}_t$ is the unemployment gap, $\alpha$ is a positive constant, and $\varepsilon_t$ is a random error term.
Equation~\ref{eq:philips_gap} displays a negative relationship between inflation and unemployment gap with a factor of $\alpha$.
The inflation rate $\pi_t$ in logarithmic space can be approximated by $\log(1 + \pi_t) = \Delta\log(P_t)$ for sufficiently small $\pi_t$. (up to 8-10\%)
The presented interaction substitutes the deflator for price levels and uses $\Delta\log(P_t^{GDP})$ as a deflator-based inflation term.
In principle, we expect (and hypothesize) that the pseudo-inflation term defined will again have a negative relationship with unemployment gap with a non-zero factor.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

We can use the Phillips Curve to connect inflation and unemployment; however, the connection of this interaction to our regression target is not yet established.
Utilizing the \textbf{Fischer Equation}, we can relate inflation to nominal interest rates, and express output as a function of inflation using the \textbf{IS Curve}.

\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        r_t &= i_t - \pi_t \\
        \Rightarrow Y_t &= Y^* - \alpha(r_t - r_t^*)
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:fischer_output}
\end{equation}

\noindent Having established $Y_t$ as a function of $\pi_t$, we can expect to see a relationship in growth rates as well.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

Next, we consider \textbf{Okun's Law}, which describes the relationship between unemployment and output (Real GDP):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{(Y_t-Y_t^*)}{Y_t^*} = -\gamma(u_t - u_t^*)
    \label{eq:okuns_law}
\end{equation}
where $Y_t$ is the actual output, $Y_t^*$ is the potential output, $\gamma$ is a positive constant, and $(u_t - u_t^*)$ is the unemployment gap.
Okun's Law can be algebraically rearranged to express $Y_t$ in levels:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \frac{Y_t}{Y_t^*} - 1 &= -\gamma(u_t - u_t^*) \\
        100\times Y_t &= Y_t^*(1 - \gamma(u_t - u_t^*))
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:okuns_levels}
\end{equation}

\vspace{\baselineskip}

\noindent The above transformation connects output to unemployment and establishes a negative relationship.
A simple yet fundamental observation is that non-zero relationships of $P_t$, and $(u_t - u_t^*)$ are clearly defined in theory.
However, we also observe that given direct relationship between unemployment gap and output gap is linearly defined without the need of the specific interaction $O_t$.
In terms of gap modelling, we could conclude that these observations makes $O_t$ directly redundant.
Yet, in a growth rate context, we believe there's benefit in testing how the Okun based dynamics are mapped outside the gap framework.(\cite{ferrentino_mathematical_2020})
Interactions defined with these variables are therefore potential candidates to explain the output growth. (Although we expect one to be of no significance.)
In any case, an empirical relationship can be analyzed to determine the differences between theoretical and observed interactions.

\subsection{Literature Review}\label{subsec:litreview}
GDP growth is a topic of constant interest and numerous studies have explored its determinants in linear, non-linear, and machine learning contexts. (\cite{maccarrone_gdp_2021}; \cite{mandel_relationship_2019})
With the two main drivers of this paper's hypotheses being the classical Philips Curve and Okun's Law; our take on GDP modelling can be called overly-conservative or outdated by modern standards.
There are interesting, modern takes on both of the relationships these drivers describe.
Although our focus is on re-visiting the long-established theories, iterations on the underlying ideas continue to prove their relevance.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

For instance, \enquote{A Growth-Augmented Phillips Curve} (\cite{tauber_growth-augmented_2020}) adds a growth term with its own coefficient to the traditional New Keynesian Phillips Curve (NKPC).
The paper then runs historical parameter estimations to jointly estimate the coefficients of unemployment gap and growth using OLS.
Published in 2020Q2, the paper does not include the COVID-19 pandemic in the estimation period. 
However, the shocks are well-within their out-of-sample forecast horizon.
With their last observation being one period before the main COVID-19 shock, the paper manages to forecast the initial deflation and subsequent bounce-back relatively well.
They predict a -35\% change in growth and a +12\% in the first response quarter.
Papers such as these show that even purely theoretical, single-equation, and linear models can still hold noteworthy explanatory and predictive power in modern settings.

\vspace{\baselineskip}

Another example of a modern approach is presented by \cite{ferrentino_mathematical_2020} where the authors propose a static mathematically derived relationship between both the Philips Curve - Output Growth and Okun's Law - Output Growth.
The paper connects the two relationships to net export dynamics via the output growth equations.
Before any estimation, they propose a NX growth estimator consisting of multiple cyclical functions including the $\sin$ and $\cos$ of output growth as regressors, instead of the standard business cycle approach of using the output gap.

\vspace{\baselineskip}
While the above papers present modern twists on the concepts this paper builds upon, they also show that Okun's Law and the Philips Curve are still perfectly valid starting points for research.
Both papers utilize OLS estimation in single-equation scenarios, starting from the most basic forms of the relationships.
The interactions and hypotheses proposed in this paper are akin to a simplified version of the above methodologies, where the focus is on testing the validity of interactions that are present in theory as empirical predictors.
